
# Equilibrium Analysis of Iodine in CCl₄-Water System
Suppose we have a system composed of carbon tetrachloride (CCl₄) and water. When elemental iodine (I₂) and iodide ions (I⁻) are added to this biphasic system, two reversible equilibria are established as follows:

## 1. Distribution Equilibrium of I₂ between CCl₄ (Organic Phase) and Aqueous Phase
This equilibrium describes the reversible transfer of iodine molecules between the non-polar CCl₄ phase and polar aqueous phase, with an equilibrium constant denoted as K₁:  

$$\ce{I2 (CCl4) <=> I2 (aq)} \quad K_1 = \frac{[\ce{I2 (aq)}]}{[\ce{I2 (CCl4)}]}$$  

Where:  
- $[\ce{I2 (aq)}]$ = Molar concentration of I₂ in the aqueous phase  
- $[\ce{I2 (CCl4)}]$ = Molar concentration of I₂ in the CCl₄ phase  

## 2. Formation Equilibrium of Triiodide Anions (I₃⁻)
Iodine molecules in the aqueous phase react with iodide ions to form triiodide anions, with an equilibrium constant denoted as K₂:  

$$\ce{I2 (aq) + I- (aq) <=> I3- (aq)} \quad K_2 = \frac{[\ce{I3- (aq)}]}{[\ce{I2 (aq)}] \cdot [\ce{I- (aq)}]}$$  

Where:  
- $[\ce{I3- (aq)}]$ = Molar concentration of I₃⁻ in the aqueous phase  
- $[\ce{I- (aq)}]$ = Molar concentration of I⁻ in the aqueous phase  

## Key Question
If water is added to dilute the entire system, will the color of the CCl₄ solution fade? Explain the underlying equilibrium shift mechanism.


Yes, you have hit the nail on the head. The fallacy is entirely caused by the **heterogeneous (biphasic) nature** of the system—specifically, the fact that "dilution" affects the volume of only one phase (water) while the other phase (CCl₄) remains unchanged.

Here is the breakdown of why the analysis fails for heterogeneous systems but works for homogeneous ones, and the rules for when you can use combined equations.

### 1. Why it failed: The "Asymmetric" Dilution
The mathematical reason your combined reaction quotient ($Q$) remained unchanged is that you treated the dilution factor asymmetrically:
*   **Aqueous species:** Concentrations dropped by half (if volume doubled).
*   **Organic species:** Concentration remained constant.

In the combined equation $\ce{I2 (CCl4) + I- (aq) <=> I3- (aq)}$:
$$Q = \frac{[\ce{I3-}]_{aq}}{[\ce{I2}]_{org} \cdot [\ce{I-}]_{aq}}$$

If we double the water volume ($V_{aq} \to 2V_{aq}$):
$$Q_{new} = \frac{0.5 \times [\ce{I3-}]_{aq}}{1.0 \times [\ce{I2}]_{org} \cdot 0.5 \times [\ce{I-}]_{aq}} = Q_{old}$$

Mathematically, $Q$ did not change. However, thermodynamics is driven by **chemical potential** ($\mu$).
*   Equilibrium requires $\mu_{\ce{I2(org)}} = \mu_{\ce{I2(aq)}}$.
*   Diluting the water lowers $\mu_{\ce{I2(aq)}}$ (making it more stable/lower energy).
*   Since the organic phase was *not* diluted, $\mu_{\ce{I2(org)}}$ stays high.
*   Matter flows from high potential to low potential (Organic $\to$ Aqueous).

The combined equation "hides" this potential difference because it cancels out the bridging species $\ce{I2 (aq)}$.

---

### 2. Can you do this in a Homogeneous System?
**Yes.** If all reactions occurred in a single aqueous phase, your method of combining equations would work perfectly.

**Example of a Homogeneous System:**
Imagine a sequential reaction all in water:
1.  $\ce{A (aq) + B (aq) <=> C (aq)}$
2.  $\ce{C (aq) <=> D (aq)}$

**Combined:** $\ce{A (aq) + B (aq) <=> D (aq)}$

If you dilute this system:
*   **All** species concentrations ([A], [B], [C], [D]) decrease by the same factor.
*   You can calculate $Q$ for the combined reaction.
*   Since there are 2 moles of solute on the left and 1 on the right, dilution will cause $Q < K$.
*   The system shifts **left** (towards A + B).

This matches what you would find if you analyzed the steps individually. In single-phase systems, the "stress" (dilution) applies universally to every species, so the net equation successfully captures the total thermodynamic stoichiometry.

---

### 3. Rules: When can you apply Combined Analysis?

You can safely sum equations and analyze the shift of the net reaction **only if** the external change (perturbation) affects the species in both "hidden" steps identically.

| Scenario | Perturbation | Combined Analysis Valid? | Why? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Homogeneous** (All Aqueous) | Dilution | **YES** | All concentrations change by the same factor $V_{final}/V_{initial}$. |
| **Homogeneous** | Temperature Change | **YES*** | Assuming you calculate the net $\Delta H$. |
| **Heterogeneous** (e.g., Gas/Solid) | Pressure/Volume Change | **YES** | Solids have constant activity ($a=1$). Gas concentrations change uniformly. |
| **Heterogeneous** (Liquid/Liquid) | Adding Solvent A (Dilution) | **NO** | Concentrations in Phase A drop; Concentrations in Phase B stay constant. The $Q$ calculation fails to account for the chemical potential gap created between phases. |

### Summary
The "Combined Equation" approach assumes that the system is a single "soup" where the reaction quotient $Q$ responds uniformly to volume changes.
*   **In your Iodine/CCl₄ system:** The "soup" is separated. Adding water dilutes the right side of the partition wall but not the left. The combined equation fails to capture the pressure across that wall.
*   **In a purely aqueous system:** The combined equation works because the entire universe of that reaction is being stretched equally.
